The Scarce Kilobyte

Three subsystems on one small chip — the radio, the memory, the screen — and the reason they cannot be understood separately: they are all spending from the same purse.

  • Channel A — network
  • Channel B — memory
  • Channel C — panel

Every confusing thing this device has ever done traces back to one fact, and it is not an obvious one. The ESP32-S3 has 8 megabytes of external PSRAM sitting almost entirely unused, and it still runs out of memory.

That sounds like a contradiction. It isn't. The chip has two kinds of memory that are not interchangeable, and the two things that matter most — putting pixels on the glass and opening an encrypted connection — can both only use the small kind. Understanding why is the whole of this document, and once you have it, no connection (-1) and out of memory stop being mysteries and become arithmetic.

We'll follow one poll from the antenna to the pixel, in three parts.

Part one — Channel AThe journey of one poll

Every eight seconds the radar asks the internet a question: what is flying within 54 nautical miles of this point? That single question is nine separate pieces of work, and only the last one is about aircraft.

  1. Associate with the access point

    The radio finds your router's network, proves it knows the password, and joins. This is radio work — no internet involved yet. It is also where wrong password? and no answer from AP come from.

    once at boot · ~2–20 s
  2. Get an address by DHCP

    The router hands out an IP address, a gateway, and the address of a DNS server. Until this lands the device is on the network but has no way to address anything on it.

    once at boot · usually < 1 s
  3. Ask what time it is

    NTP, over UDP. It matters more than it looks: without a real clock the device cannot check whether a TLS certificate has expired, and it cannot label the traffic history it is learning.

    once at boot, then hourly
  4. Resolve the hostname

    api.adsb.lol is a name, not a destination. DNS turns it into an IP address. A device that has joined Wi-Fi but cannot resolve names looks exactly like a device with no internet at all.

    cached after the first lookup
  5. Open a TCP connection

    The three-way handshake: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. Now there is a reliable pipe between the chip and a server somewhere in Europe. Anything sent through it arrives in order, or not at all.

    one round trip · ~20–60 ms
  6. Negotiate TLS

    This is the expensive step, and it is expensive in memory rather than time. The next section is about nothing else.

    two round trips · ~40 kB of internal RAM
  7. Send the HTTP request

    A dozen lines of text — the path, the host, the User-Agent that identifies this device honestly to a volunteer-run service. Encrypted by step six, so nobody between here and there can read it.

    a few hundred bytes
  8. Receive the JSON

    Up to a couple of hundred kilobytes of aircraft data, arriving in 16 kB chunks called TLS records. Each one is decrypted as it lands.

    ~10–200 kB · buffered in PSRAM
  9. Parse and keep 96

    A filter throws away every field the device never draws before the parser builds anything. What survives is at most 96 aircraft, sorted by distance — and then every value is checked for physical plausibility before a single page is allowed to see it.

    ~15 ms

What TLS actually does, and why it costs so much

TLS is what puts the s in https. It does three things at once: proves the server is who it claims to be, agrees a secret key nobody watching can derive, and then encrypts everything afterwards.

The proving part is a certificate — a document signed by an authority the device already trusts, saying "this really is api.adsb.lol". The agreeing part is a key exchange, some elliptic-curve arithmetic that lets two parties end up holding the same secret number without ever sending it. Both are fast on this chip. Neither is the problem.

The problem is the third part. Once the handshake is done, TLS doesn't encrypt a stream — it encrypts records, and the standard says a record may be up to 16 kilobytes. The receiver has no way to know how big the next one will be, so it must have a full 16 kB buffer ready before it arrives. And because the connection is two-way, it needs another one for outgoing records.

incoming record buffer16,384 bytes outgoing record buffer16,384 bytes session state, certificate chain, working space~8,000 bytes total, all of it from internal memory≈ 40 kB

Forty kilobytes, every time it opens a connection. Hold that number.

Part two — Channel BTwo kinds of memory

The ESP32-S3 in this device has two memory pools, and the difference between them is the single most important fact about the whole build.

Internal SRAM — 512 kB. On the same silicon as the processor. Fast, and reachable by every part of the chip including the DMA engine. This is the scarce one.

External PSRAM — 8 MB. A separate chip, wired alongside the processor over a high-speed octal bus. Enormous by comparison, and fine for bulk data. But it is not on the CPU die, and some hardware simply cannot reach it.

Not all 512 kB of the internal pool is available, either. The ROM bootloader, the Wi-Fi driver, the TCP stack, FreeRTOS, and every task's stack all take their share first. What's left for everything else is roughly 320 kB at boot, and less once the radio is running.

Why free memory is not the same as usable memory

There are about 207 kB free in the internal pool, and TLS wants 40. That looks comfortable. It isn't, because of fragmentation.

Memory gets handed out and given back constantly, and the gaps left behind are scattered. You can have 207 kB free in total and still not have 16 kB in one unbroken run — the same way a car park with 200 free spaces can have nowhere to put a bus. What matters for a big allocation is not free memory, it is the largest contiguous free block, and on this device while it is running that number sits around 31.7 kB.

Two 16 kB buffers do not fit in 31.7 kB.

The handshake lottery

This is why the same device fetches perfectly for a minute and then fails four times in a row. Whether a handshake succeeds depends on what the heap happened to look like that second — whether a big enough gap had opened up. It is a coin flip, not a fault.

The firmware's answer is not to fix the memory (it can't) but to try three times, 700 ms apart. Three coin flips instead of one takes the odds of an empty poll from roughly 1-in-2 to about 1-in-8. The pause matters too: the previous attempt's buffers are only released when its connection object goes out of scope, and the heap needs a moment to merge those freed pieces back into usable runs.

And it is why the aircraft limit is 96 rather than 256, why the behaviour tracker lives in PSRAM, and why 7.6 MB of PSRAM sitting idle is a design decision rather than waste. Every one of those choices is protecting the same 31.7 kB.

Part three — Channel CThe wire to the glass

The round display is a GC9A01: 240×240 pixels, driven over SPI. Seven wires connect it to the processor, and the two that are missing explain more about this project than the five that are there.

The arithmetic of one frame

Each pixel is two bytes — five bits of red, six of green, five of blue. The green gets the extra bit because the eye is most sensitive to it.

240 × 240 pixels × 2 bytes115,200 B · 112.5 kB × 8 bits, ÷ 80 Mbit/s11.5 ms per frame A hard ceiling of about 87 frames per second — before a single pixel has been drawn.

Eleven and a half milliseconds of pure transmission, every frame, and the processor cannot spend that time waiting. This is what DMA is for: a separate engine that pushes the buffer out over SPI while the CPU gets on with the next frame.

Why the framebuffer cannot live in the big memory

The obvious fix to all of part two is to move the 112.5 kB framebuffer into the 8 MB of PSRAM. It doesn't work, and the reason is hardware: the DMA engine can only address internal SRAM.

Point the framebuffer at PSRAM and the graphics library quietly disables DMA — it has no choice — and every frame becomes a 112.5 kB copy performed by the processor itself. The CRT-style phosphor fade, which walks all 57,600 pixels every frame, slows by five to ten times. The screen visibly stutters.

So the framebuffer stays in the scarce pool, and TLS has to negotiate for what's left. That is the collision this whole document is about.

Why no MISO changed the software

SPI normally has a return wire. This panel doesn't use one, so the processor can write to the display but never read from it — it has no idea what is currently on screen.

That sounds academic until you try to draw something semi-transparent. Blending a 40%-opaque line means combining its colour with whatever is underneath, which means knowing what is underneath. Most graphics libraries do that by reading the destination pixel back. On this panel that read returns nothing.

The way round it is to keep a complete copy of the screen in the chip's own memory, do every blend against that copy, and push the finished result out. Which is exactly what the framebuffer is — and why it is not optional, and why it is 112.5 kB, and why it is sitting in the memory TLS wanted.

Every arrow in this document points back to the same place.

Part fourReading the symptoms

With the three channels in mind, the messages on the System page stop being a list to memorise and become a diagnosis.

What you seeWhich channelWhat is actually happening
no connection (-1)A + BThe handshake lost the lottery — no 16 kB block was free at that moment. Once or twice at boot is normal. Constantly means the signal is too weak to complete a handshake before it times out, which is a different cause with the same symptom.
out of memoryBNot fragmentation — the 192 kB JSON buffer could not be allocated at all. On this chip that means PSRAM was never switched on in the build, and the device stops rather than half-working.
PSRAM off in buildBThe same thing, named exactly. The extra memory chip needs a specific setting; get it wrong and the chip boots, runs, and reports zero bytes of PSRAM with no error at all.
feed refused (403)ANothing to do with memory. The server understood the request perfectly and declined to answer it.
JSON failAThe reply arrived damaged — usually a connection dropped mid-transfer on a weak link, leaving a truncated document.
Screen tearing or stutterCFrames taking longer than their slot. If it appeared after a code change, something is being drawn per-pixel that used to be drawn per-shape.
FETCH OK below 90%ASignal, nine times out of ten. Handshakes need several round trips and a marginal link fails them faster than it fails anything else.

The one paragraph to remember

The panel cannot be read from, so the device must keep a full copy of the screen in memory. That copy must be in internal SRAM, because the DMA engine cannot reach anywhere else. Internal SRAM is the same 512 kB pool that the encrypted connection needs 40 contiguous kilobytes from. The display and the network are competing for the same scarce kilobyte, and every design decision in this project is a referee.
