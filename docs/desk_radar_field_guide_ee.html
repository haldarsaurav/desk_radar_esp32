Desk Radar · rev5.2 · field guide

Desk Radar Field Guide

A round screen on a desk that shows the aircraft actually overhead, right now, from a live feed. This is everything on it: what every mark means, what every colour means, and how each number is worked out.

Your radar, specifically

The scope is centred on you — on whatever position you put into the setup page, in Tartu. Every distance, every bearing and every “nearest aircraft” on this device is measured from that point, and it is the one thing worth getting roughly right.

The airport page is centred on Tallinn, Lennart Meri (EETN), about 160 km north-west of you. It has its own separate zoom, draws the single runway 08/26, and the weather page shows Tallinn's own hourly report. The two pages are genuinely two different views, not one relabelled.

Because the airport is that far away, the radar quietly asks for a wider circle of aircraft than you set — enough to cover both places — so the airport page is never empty for want of data. You never have to think about it.

01What it is

A small radar scope that is not pretending. Every aircraft on it is a real aircraft, in the sky, at that moment.

Airliners continuously broadcast their identity, position, altitude, heading and speed over the radio — a system called ADS-B. Volunteers around the world run receivers that pick those broadcasts up and pool them. This device asks that pool, every eight seconds, “what is within so many kilometres of here?” and draws the answer.

It is not a flight tracking app on a screen. It is a single instrument that does one thing continuously, in the corner of a room, with no notifications and nothing to tap. You look up, you see the sky as a picture, you look away.

The hardware is an ESP32-S3 microcontroller and a 240 × 240 round colour display, about the size of a large wristwatch face. It needs power over USB and a Wi-Fi network. Nothing else.

What it cannot do

It only sees aircraft that broadcast ADS-B and are within range of a volunteer receiver. Light aircraft, gliders and most military traffic often do not appear. Low-level traffic behind hills may not appear. An empty scope at three in the morning usually means an empty sky — but not always.

02Switching it on

The first time it starts, it has no Wi-Fi and creates its own network instead. Setting it up takes about two minutes on a phone.

  1. Plug it in. Any USB power supply. The screen lights within a couple of seconds and shows a QR code.
  2. Point your phone camera at the QR code. Your phone offers to join a network called DESK-RADAR-xxxx. Join it. If the camera does not offer, join that network by hand from Wi-Fi settings — it has no password.
  3. The setup page opens by itself. If it does not, open a browser and go to 192.168.4.1.
  4. Choose your Wi-Fi and enter the password, then press Test this connection. The device joins your real network and tells you whether it worked, before you commit to anything.
  5. Set where you are. Long-press your home in any maps app to get the two numbers, or press Find my position, which is accurate to a few kilometres — plenty at this scale.
  6. Press Save & restart. The setup network disappears, the device joins yours, and aircraft appear within about ten seconds.

Everything is stored on the device. There is no account, no app, and nothing is sent anywhere except the requests for aircraft and weather data.

By default the pages also change themselves every twelve seconds. You can turn that off, change the interval, or switch off any page you never want to see.

One thing not to do

Do not hold the button down while plugging the USB cable in. That pin doubles as the chip's programming pin: held down at power-on, the device waits to be reflashed and the screen stays black. Unplug, wait, plug back in without touching it.

Aircraft are coloured by altitude

On the radar page each aircraft is tinted by how high it is, so the scope reads as a volume rather than a flat map. Low traffic is warm, high traffic is cool. This is a smooth blend, not steps — an aircraft at 8,000 ft is half way between the yellow and the green.

So: orange is circuit height or on approach, yellow is a small aircraft or a jet still climbing out, green is mid-level, cyan and blue are cruising airliners, and pink is above them — business jets, and occasionally something unusual.

Turning it off

The setup page has a Colour by altitude switch. With it off, aircraft are coloured green when descending and amber when climbing instead, which some people find easier to read. The altitude ramp is on by default because it carries more information.

05Shapes and marks

Colour says what kind of thing it is. Shape says what it is doing.

Chevron
An aircraft in the air. The point of the chevron is the direction it is travelling, and it is rotated to the aircraft's real heading. It is always drawn pointing where the aircraft is going, not where it came from.
Fading tail behind a chevron
Where it has been over roughly the last eight seconds. This is not a stored track — it is the previous frames of the picture fading out, the way an old radar tube behaved. Longer tail means the display's trail setting is longer, not that the aircraft is faster.
Thin line out ahead of a chevron
A velocity leader: where the aircraft will be shortly if it keeps its current speed and heading. A long line means it is moving fast.
Small violet dot
Something on the ground — parked at a stand or taxiing. A short stub off the dot means it is moving.
Faint dot on the rim
An aircraft the feed knows about that is beyond the edge of the current range. It is pinned to the rim rather than dropped, so you can see traffic is about to arrive.
Ring around a chevron
This one is doing something unusual. The ring's colour says what: red a go-around or a very fast descent, amber holding, violet circling in one place, lilac flying a survey pattern. The Patterns page names them.
Red triangle at the top
North. It is the only red thing on an ordinary screen, and it appears on every page that has an orientation.
Slowly rotating line
The sweep, running from the middle out to the edge once every ten seconds. It is decoration — a nod to a real radar — and deliberately dim, drawn underneath everything else so it can never obscure an aircraft. Aircraft do not appear as it passes; they appear when the data does.
Short bright arc travelling round the rim
Not the sweep, and easy to confuse with it. It is the health ring: its colour is the device's status and its movement is the proof that the screen has not frozen. A still device draws a still ring.

06The eleven pages

Each page answers a different question. They cycle automatically, or you can step through them with the button.

1 · Radar — what is overhead

The main page, and the one it sits on most. You are at the centre. North is up. Everything is drawn where it actually is relative to you.

The rings
Three of them, at a quarter, a half and three quarters of the range. The number on the lower-right diagonal is the outer edge — here 50 km — and the smaller numbers inside it are the inner rings. State the unit once at the edge, the rest are understood.
Number at the top
How many aircraft are airborne inside the circle you can see.
Number on the right (east)
How many of them are descending — arriving.
Number on the left (west)
How many are climbing — departing.
Clock at the bottom
Local time, from the internet. It is the quickest way to tell the device is still alive.
Labels beside some aircraft
Callsign, altitude and speed. Only a few are labelled — three by default — because a scope with a label on every contact is unreadable. Aircraft on the ground are never labelled.
AUTO
Appears only if the automatic range feature is switched on. See how the range chooses itself.
A line of text near the bottom
Appears only when the scope is empty, and says why in the feed's own words — “0 in 50 km” means the sky really is empty; “HTTP 429” means the data source asked us to slow down.

How an aircraft gets to its place on the glass

The feed gives latitude and longitude. Those are converted to kilometres east and north of your position — one degree of latitude is always about 111 km, and one degree of longitude is that times the cosine of your latitude, which is why the conversion needs to know where you are. The result is scaled so the outer ring equals the chosen range, and drawn. North stays up and the screen is never rotated.

Between updates the aircraft does not freeze. Its last known position, heading and speed are used to carry it forward, so the picture keeps moving smoothly at 40 frames a second while the data arrives every eight seconds.

2 · Airport — the field, close up

The same sky, zoomed right in on one airport, with the runways drawn. This page is centred on the airport, not on you.

Two columns under the name
The arrival and the departure that matter most — the one closest to landing and the one that most recently left. Each shows callsign, distance, altitude and speed, and a line reaches down to the aircraft it describes. If a box seems to stay when the aircraft levels off, that is deliberate: it holds the last one rather than blinking out.
Grey strips
The runways, drawn to scale, at their real orientation. The thinner lines beside them are taxiways, and the small slab in the middle is the terminal.
Arrow on a runway end
Which way the field is landing. Green means it was worked out from the actual wind; amber means there was no weather report and it was inferred from what the arriving traffic is doing — still usually right, but a guess.
Dotted line running out from a threshold
The final approach path, about 10 km of it, with dots drifting inwards. It shows where landing traffic will be.
Numbers at east, west and south
Arrivals, departures, and how many aircraft are on the ground.
A bordered box that moves
The one label allowed to float. It appears beside anything notable — a heavy jet, something military, a helicopter, an emergency — and says what makes it notable.
Bottom line
Temperature, then wind direction and speed, straight from the airport's weather report.

How it knows which way the field is landing

Aircraft land into wind. The device takes the wind direction from the weather report and picks whichever runway end faces closest to it — the arrow goes there, in green. If there is no weather report it falls back to watching: the average heading of aircraft that are descending near the field points at the runway in use, and the arrow turns amber to say the answer was inferred rather than measured.

Ground traffic, three ways

A contact on the surface is drawn as an amber chevron if it is on a runway — something is rolling, and that is the interesting case — as a violet dot with a short stub if it is taxiing, and as a dimmer violet dot with nothing if it is parked.

3 · Traffic — how busy is it

One big number and the shape of the last hour.

The headline
How many aircraft are airborne within the counted radius, which is named on the line underneath so there is never any doubt.
The trace
Sixty samples, one a minute: the last hour. The number to its left is the tallest point on the trace, so a flat line at 3 and a flat line at 30 do not look identical. The dot at the right-hand end is now, and it turns amber when the sky is filling and green when it is emptying.
Six rows
Climbing, descending, above 30,000 ft, on the ground, helicopters, military. Each has a small bar so the proportions read without doing arithmetic.
The starburst at the bottom
Where the traffic is, as a compass rose: eight spokes, each as long as its share. The lit one is the busiest direction and the caption names it. On an even sky every spoke is the same length and it reads as a circle — which is itself the answer.

Why the sparkline is smooth

The count wobbles by two or three between polls simply because aircraft drop in and out at the edge of the search radius. A sample taken on the minute would carry all of that wobble. Instead the count is accumulated twice a second and the average for each clock minute is stored, so only a real change of traffic shows as a slope.

4 · Nearest — the closest aircraft

One aircraft, in detail: whichever is physically closest to you.

Callsign
The flight number it is broadcasting, e.g. DLH2AB for a Lufthansa flight. Not the same as the commercial flight number you would find on a ticket.
Distance and bearing
Straight-line ground distance from you, and the compass direction to look in.
Altitude, speed, vertical rate
Feet above sea level, knots over the ground, and feet per minute up or down.
Type, airline and route
Looked up separately, and only for the featured aircraft. If the lookup has not answered yet, or the aircraft is not in the database, those lines show a dash rather than the previous aircraft's details.
The grey line at the bottom
“closest of 25 within 100 km” — what this aircraft was chosen from, and out of how many. Every page that measures a circle says which circle, so two pages can never quietly disagree about what is out there.
NO CONTACT
Means nothing is inside your sky — the fixed circle the radar counts over, 100 km unless you changed it. That circle does not shrink when the scope zooms in, so this page keeps working on a quiet night. An aircraft further out than your sky is still tracked; it is simply not called “the nearest”.

5 · Coolest — the most interesting aircraft

The same layout, but the aircraft is chosen by how unusual it is rather than how close.

How the winner is picked
Every aircraft gets a score. Doing something unusual is worth the most; being military, a helicopter, or a type the device has never seen before all add heavily; height adds a little. Highest score wins.
Never seen before
The device remembers the last few hundred aircraft it has seen. Something that has never appeared before scores well — which is what surfaces the once-a-month visitor rather than the same commuter flight every morning.

It reaches as far as your sky, the same fixed circle Nearest uses — so it will happily show you something 80 km away that the scope is currently too zoomed-in to draw. That is the point: it is telling you what is around, not what fits on the glass.

6 · Space — overhead, much higher up

The International Space Station, and what is launching next.

The dome
The sky above you, seen from below. The rim is the horizon, the centre is straight up, and the ring between them is 30° elevation.
The moving dot
The ISS, where it is now, with a solid fading track behind it and a dotted one ahead showing where it is heading. The track always covers about the same slice of sky whether the station is passing overhead or crawling along the horizon, so its length tells you nothing — its direction is the point.
BELOW HORIZON
Most of the time. A marker just outside the rim still shows which direction it is in, breathing slowly, so the page is never completely dead.
VISIBLE NOW
Three things at once: it is above your horizon, it is in sunlight, and it is dark where you are. That combination is what makes it a bright moving star rather than an invisible one.
The lower panel
The next four rocket launches worldwide, rotating every six seconds with a slow cross-fade. The name is the rocket and its payload — Falcon 9 | Starlink 12-4 is a Falcon 9 carrying Starlink satellites. Under it is the operator, coloured by agency, and a countdown.
T+120:40
Time to launch, in hours and minutes. T− counts down to it; T+ means the scheduled time has passed — launches slip constantly, and the feed does not always update at once.

7 · Weather — the airport report

The real weather observation from the airport, decoded. This is not a forecast and not a general city weather feed — it is the report pilots use, issued about once an hour.

VFR / MVFR / IFR / LIFR
The flight category, and the single most useful line: how good the visibility and cloud base are. VFR is clear, MVFR marginal, IFR poor, and LIFR is genuinely bad. The ring around the page carries the same colour.
The wind rose
An arrow pointing from where the wind is blowing towards the centre — the meteorological convention, because a “westerly” comes from the west. The number below reads direction/speed, so 260/12 is from 260° at 12 knots. A G adds gusts. A ring of inward ticks instead of an arrow means the wind is variable with no settled direction.
TEMP / DEW
Temperature and dew point in Celsius. When the two are within about two degrees the air is nearly saturated — mist and fog weather — and the row turns amber.
CLOUD
The lowest broken or overcast layer and its height in feet. BKN is broken, OVC overcast, SCT scattered, CLR clear.
QNH
Air pressure at sea level in hectopascals. Around 1013 is average; much lower means a depression.
obs N min ago
How old the observation is — not how long since the device downloaded it. Reports are hourly, so anything under about an hour is current.

8 · Patterns — who is doing something odd

Most aircraft fly in a straight line. This page lists the ones that are not, and it works it out from the movement itself — nothing in the feed says “this aircraft is holding”.

HOLDING
Flying a racetrack: a full circle or more of turn inside a small area, at a steady height, at airliner speed. It usually means the airport is backed up.
GO-AROUND
An aborted landing. Came down to approach height near the airport and then climbed away again. Rare, and the single most interesting thing this device catches.
CIRCLING
A tight sustained turn over one spot, slowly. Police, air ambulance, news helicopters.
SURVEY
A lawnmower pattern — long parallel legs with turns at each end. Mapping, aerial photography, pipeline inspection.
EMERG DESC
Descending faster than 3,500 ft per minute and has lost a lot of height. Usually a rapid descent for an ordinary reason, occasionally not.
NOTHING UNUSUAL
The normal state, and it says how many aircraft it is watching so an empty page still shows the device is working.

How a pattern is recognised

The device keeps the last 48 positions of up to 64 aircraft — about a hundred seconds of movement each. From that it measures how much the aircraft has turned in total, how far it actually flew against how far it ended up from where it started, the size of the box it stayed inside, and how much its altitude varied.

A hold turns more than 300° but ends up near where it began, inside a small box, at a steady height, without ever reversing sharply. A survey pattern also turns a lot and goes nowhere, but reverses hard at the end of every leg — that reversal count is what tells the two apart. Circling is the same as a hold but in a much smaller box and much slower, which is a helicopter rather than a jet.

A go-around is the hardest, because a normal departure looks similar: low, then climbing. The difference is the shape of the altitude trace. A departure only ever goes up — its lowest point is the oldest thing it remembers. A go-around is a V: down to approach height, then up again, with the low point in the middle. That, plus being near the airport, is the test.

9 · Trends — is today normal

The only page that needs time to become useful. It compares right now against what this particular device has seen at the same hour of the same weekday, over however many weeks it has been running.

LEARNING
What it says for the first week or so, with a progress bar and the date it started. It genuinely cannot answer the question until it has seen a full week.
The verdict
BUSIER THAN USUAL, NORMAL, QUIETER THAN USUAL, and so on, in a colour matching how far from normal it is.
usually 18 · +1.5 sigma
What it normally expects at this hour, and how far today is from that, in standard deviations. Roughly: under 1 is ordinary, 1–2 is noticeably different, over 2 is unusual.
The bar chart
The last 24 hours. Bars are what actually happened; the pale line across them is what this device expected for those hours. Where the bars sit above the line, it was busier than usual.
12 wks of this hour · since 6 Aug
How much evidence stands behind the verdict, and when it started learning. Two devices can both read “38% of a week seen” and be three days and three weeks old — only the date tells them apart.

The setup page has a Restart learning button. Use it after moving the device or changing how far it looks, because until you do it is judging today against a week that no longer exists.

10 · System — is it healthy

The page to look at when something seems wrong, and safe to ignore otherwise.

Signal bars
Wi-Fi strength. Four bars is excellent, three is good, two is where to start suspecting the radio rather than the data. The number beside them is in dBm and is negative: −55 is strong, −70 is fair, −85 is marginal.
FETCH OK
The percentage of data requests that have succeeded since it was switched on. It is a lifetime average, so a bad patch this morning still shows at lunchtime. Below about 80% is worth investigating; the reason is almost always Wi-Fi signal.
The feed line
What the aircraft feed last said, in its own words. “12 aircraft” is healthy. “0 in 50 km” means it answered and the sky is empty.
Free memory
Two numbers. The small one is the memory that matters and is genuinely scarce; the large one is a separate pool that mostly stays free by design, and is not a fault.
Frame time
How long a screen takes to draw. Anything under about 25 ms is comfortable.
Uptime
How long since it last restarted. Red for the first few minutes after a restart, which is how you spot one you did not expect.

11 · Credits

The only page with no data on it. Three chevrons orbit at rates that are not multiples of each other, so the picture never quite repeats; a slow ping goes out from the middle behind them; and the text breathes rather than blinking.

This one is a dedication rather than a credit. It reads made for Agnes & Arko, and it is the reason this build exists at all.

The whole page can be switched off in the setup page too — a device given to someone else should not insist on crediting its builder every time the cycle comes round.

07Screens that are not pages

Starting up

Before the first aircraft arrive, the device shows a card rather than an empty scope. It names the network it is joining and the step it has reached, so a device that gets stuck says where it got stuck instead of showing a generic “connecting” forever.

If it cannot join at all it says so and opens the setup network by itself.

An emergency squawk

Aircraft can set one of three codes on their transponder to say something is wrong: 7500 unlawful interference, 7600 radio failure, 7700 general emergency. If one appears within range, a red banner takes over whatever page is showing and the status ring pulses.

It is rare, and 7600 — a broken radio — is by far the most common of the three. It can be switched off entirely in the setup page.

08How it is worked out

Everything on the screen is computed on the device itself from raw position reports. Nothing arrives pre-drawn.

Distance and bearing

Distances use the haversine formula on a sphere of radius 6,371 km, which is accurate to a fraction of a percent at these scales — far inside the error of the position reports themselves. Bearings are true, not magnetic: 0° is north, 90° east.

Altitude

Altitudes are what the aircraft broadcasts, which is pressure altitude on the standard 1013.25 hPa setting rather than height above the ground. Above about 18,000 ft everyone uses that same setting so the numbers are directly comparable; below it, on a low-pressure day, an aircraft's true height can differ by several hundred feet. FL300 means 30,000 ft on that standard setting.

The velocity leader

The line ahead of each aircraft is where it will be after a fixed number of seconds. That number is not fixed in the way you might expect: it scales with the range. On a 10 km scope it projects about 12 seconds ahead, on a 90 km scope about 90 seconds. Without that, every leader on a close-in scope would run off the edge and they would all look identical — which is exactly the fault it was written to fix.

Your sky, and why it is not the scope

There are three different distances inside this device, and they answer three different questions. Confusing them is the single easiest way to misread the screen, so they are kept apart deliberately.

The range
What fits on the glass. The outer ring of the radar page. It is a drawing decision — forty aircraft on a 240-pixel circle is unreadable, so on a busy afternoon you want a tight scope. Change it freely; it changes only the picture.
Your sky
What counts as “around here”. A fixed circle, 100 km by default, set once in the setup page. It decides the big number on the Traffic page and everything under it, and how far Nearest and Coolest may look. It never moves on its own.
The request
What the device asks the internet for. Wider than both, so aircraft are already being tracked before they reach the outer ring, and wide enough to cover the airport page as well. You never see this number and never need to.

Your sky used to follow the range, and it was wrong twice over. At three in the morning the scope zooms in because the sky is quiet — and Nearest and Coolest would both go blank, the radar announcing “nothing is out there” when it meant “nothing is inside the circle I happen to be drawing”. Worse, Trends compares this Tuesday with the Tuesdays before it, and that comparison only means anything if every week was measured over the same sky. A radius that moved with a display setting quietly made weeks of learning incomparable, with nothing on screen to say so.

So: set your sky once, then forget it, and zoom the scope as much as you like.

The range choosing itself

Optional, and off unless you switch it on. Every 20 seconds the device counts how many aircraft sit inside each range it could use — it already has the data, so this costs nothing — and picks the smallest range that still holds a workable picture. On a quiet night that is the wide end; on a busy afternoon it is the close end.

The safeguards matter more than the rule:

  • It moves one step at a time, at most once an hour. That is deliberately slow: anything that moves on a device sitting in a room gets noticed, and air traffic changes over hours anyway. You should never catch it happening.
  • The sky must have looked different for ten minutes before anything moves.
  • Switching the radar on is the exception — it goes straight to the right range, because nobody is startled by an instrument that is already correct.
  • It will not move while the data feed is stale. A dead feed reports an empty sky, and reacting to that would park the scope at maximum range until you noticed.
  • It will not move during an emergency.
  • The word AUTO appears on the radar page, so a scope that reads differently tomorrow is never a mystery.

Two centres, one request

The radar page is centred on you and draws your chosen range around you. The airport page is centred on the airport and draws its own, separate scope around that. The weather comes from that airport's own report. They are genuinely two different views, not one view relabelled.

But both are fed by a single request for aircraft, because a single request is all the memory allows. While the airport is nearby that is invisible — a circle drawn around your home contains it comfortably. When the airport is far away it stops being invisible: a 100 km request around home simply does not reach an airport 165 km off, and the airport page would then be empty forever, looking exactly like a broken feed.

So the device sizes the request to contain both. If your radius already covers what the airport page needs, nothing changes. If it does not, the request slides to the midpoint between the two and grows just enough to reach both ends. That is also less data than simply inflating the home radius until it reached, and it puts both places the same distance from the centre of the request — which matters, because only the nearest few dozen aircraft are kept, and an airport left out at the edge would be the first to be dropped.

The learned baseline

Once an hour, the device stores the average number of aircraft it saw during that hour, filed under which hour of which weekday it was — 168 slots for a week. Over time each slot builds a mean and a spread. “Busier than usual” means today's count is more than about one standard deviation above that slot's mean.

This is why the count it learns from is deliberately pinned to a fixed radius and does not follow the automatic range. A model fed a count whose radius keeps changing learns the zoom, not the sky.

Sunrise, sunset, and whether the ISS is visible

Sun times use the standard NOAA solar position algorithm from your latitude, longitude and the date — accurate to about a minute, and needing no network. The ISS is called visible only when it is more than 10° above your horizon, in sunlight, and dark where you are. Its elevation comes from its ground distance and its altitude, allowing for the curve of the Earth.

What the weather report actually says

The device reads a METAR — the standard hourly airport observation. Two traps it handles that are worth knowing about: visibility is sometimes reported as text (“10+”) rather than a number, and pressure comes in different units in different countries. Both are converted before anything is displayed, so you never see a misleading zero.

10The setup page

Reopen it any time by holding the button for about a second, or tapping RESET twice. The device restarts into its own network; join it and the page opens.

Where you are

The most important setting. Everything on every page is measured from this point. It is your position, not the airport's.

The scope

Range is a trade, not a quality setting. 10–20 km is a handful of aircraft you can follow individually. 50 km is the best all-round choice. 100 km and beyond puts forty contacts on a small circle and tells you how busy the sky is but nothing about any single flight in it.

How far to look for aircraft

Keep this comfortably larger than the range above — about double — so aircraft are already being tracked before they cross the outer ring. Do not simply maximise it: the amount of data grows with the square of this number.

It means “how far around you”, and that is all you need it to mean. If the reference airport is further away than this, the device notices and quietly widens the request to cover both places — you never have to raise this number to make the airport page work. See two centres, one request.

Refresh interval

Eight seconds is the sweet spot. Slower does not make the picture jerky, because aircraft are carried forward between updates; it just makes them slightly stale. Faster is not better either — the sources only update every few seconds anyway, and each extra request is work the little chip has to find memory for. Most connection problems trace back to this being set too low.

Asking the radar how it is getting on

You do not have to interpret any of this yourself. Hold the button to open the setup page and scroll to Health: the radar keeps a running count of how its requests have been going, reads it back, and tells you in plain words — “the Wi-Fi signal is too weak, move it closer to the router” — along with the numbers behind that conclusion. The counts survive a restart, so what you see is the state of the run just before you opened the page.

Underneath there is a link to the full log: every line the radar has written recently. It rolls over as it goes, so it can never grow large and what is in it is always recent. You should not need it, but if you ever ask someone for help it is the thing to send them.

Going deeper

If you want to know why the encrypted connection is short of memory when the chip has eight megabytes spare, and why that is the same reason the screen has no brightness control — there is a companion document, The Scarce Kilobyte, that follows one request from the antenna to the pixel. It is not needed to use the radar. It is there because the answer is genuinely interesting.

The first minute after switching on

Give it a minute before deciding anything is wrong. In order, the radar has to join Wi-Fi, ask the internet what time it is, open an encrypted connection to the aircraft feed, and fetch a picture — and that encrypted connection needs a contiguous block of memory that is genuinely scarce. A failed first attempt is ordinary; it tries three times in quick succession and then again eight seconds later.

The same thing can happen at any point afterwards, because every request opens its own connection and each one has to find that memory again. So an occasional no connection (-1) hours into a run is the same ordinary event, not a new fault.

What matters is the FETCH OK percentage on the System page after it has been running a while. Above 98% is healthy; below about 90% means it is too far from the router.

Why 403 in particular, and why it fixes itself

403 means “I know exactly what you asked for and I am not giving it to you.” That is the whole difference between 403 and the errors that look similar: 404 means the thing is not there, a timeout means nobody answered, but 403 means somebody answered deliberately and said no. Nothing is broken.

If you saw this before August 2026, here is what it was. The backup feed used to be a service called airplanes.live, which in April 2026 quietly stopped serving anyone who was not also running their own receiver — a non-feeder request just gets refused, sometimes with a confusing “API key invalid”. Most guides on the internet still recommend it, because they were written before the change. The backup feed is now adsb.fi, which is keyless like the main one, and this should not happen again.

The other cause is a daily allowance. The radar asks for a fresh picture every eight seconds — about 10,800 requests a day. Both feeds it uses now are happy with that (adsb.fi publishes a limit of one request per second, so the radar sits about twelve times under it), but a feed that imposed a daily cap could still refuse a device that camped on it.

The firmware treats a 403 as an answer rather than a glitch. It stops asking for about seven minutes, and it will not go back to that feed for six hours, because a daily allowance resets on the feed's clock and there is nothing to gain by asking again sooner. Hammering a service that has just said no is what turns a temporary refusal into a permanent one, so the pause is the point.

The backup feed is only ever visited for a few minutes at a time. This is the part that actually stops the problem happening. The radar used to switch to the backup when the main feed hiccuped and then simply stay there — nothing brought it home while things were working, which is precisely the state that quietly spends a day's allowance. Now the visit is capped at five minutes and the radar always returns to the main feed. Over a full day, with the main feed failing six separate times, that works out at about 220 requests to the backup against an allowance of 500 — and no refusals at all.

If you still see it, the fix is to ask less often: raise Fetch interval in the setup page from 8 seconds to 15 or 30. The scope updates a little less often and everything else is unchanged.

13Glossary

ADS-B
Automatic Dependent Surveillance – Broadcast. Aircraft broadcasting their own position and identity, continuously, unencrypted. It is what makes all of this possible.
Callsign
What an aircraft calls itself on the radio, e.g. DLH2AB. Related to but not the same as the commercial flight number.
Squawk
A four-digit code the pilot sets on the transponder. Three of them — 7500, 7600, 7700 — mean an emergency.
Knot
One nautical mile per hour, about 1.85 km/h. An airliner cruises around 450–500 knots.
Flight level
Altitude in hundreds of feet on the standard pressure setting. FL300 is 30,000 ft.
METAR
The standard airport weather observation, issued about once an hour in a heavily abbreviated format. The Weather page decodes it.
QNH
Air pressure reduced to sea level, in hectopascals. Set on an altimeter, it makes it read height above sea level.
VFR / IFR
Visual and instrument flight rules. As a weather category, VFR means good enough to fly by looking out of the window; IFR means it is not.
Go-around
An abandoned landing. The aircraft climbs away and comes back round. Routine, safe, and interesting to catch.
Hold
Flying a racetrack pattern while waiting for a landing slot.
Heavy
A wake-turbulence category for large aircraft — 747, 777, A380 and similar. The device uses it to spot the notable ones.
